The number of retracted scientific publications has risen dramatically recently, prompting questions about the underlying causes of this trend. To explore this issue, we analyzed 2,047 retracted articles indexed in PubMed and found that the average time interval between publication and retraction (time-to-retraction) was 32.91 months. Additionally, articles published before 2002 took 49.82 months but those published after 2002 took 23.82 months. However, the possibility remains that recent flawed articles may not yet have been recognized for retraction.


To assess the role of increased scrutiny, we examined the relationship between time-to-retraction and journal impact factor (IF). While high-IF journals tended to retract articles more quickly, it explained only 1% of the variance in retraction time. The correlation was stronger for articles retracted due to misconduct, but still modest overall. 


Our analysis also revealed that plagiarism and duplicate publication have become a recent cause for retraction but still relatively uncommon compared to those for fraud. Additionally, the study found that retractions are more likely to occur earlier in the article’s life cycle, with a progressive decline in time-to-retraction for articles published since 2000 which points to lowered barriers to retraction.


Moreover, behavior of authors with a single retraction become more prominent recently. From 1993 to 2012, 63.1% of retracted papers were written by authors with only one retraction, compared to 46% from 1972 to 1992 (p < 0.001). In contrast, authors with multiple retractions had a greater influence in the earlier period but contributed less proportionally to the recent surge in retractions. This shift suggests that retractions are now more broadly distributed across the scientific community rather than being concentrated among a few prolific offenders.


In conclusion, the rise in retractions reflects changes in the behavior of both authors and institutions. Lower barriers to publication of flawed articles are evidenced by the increase in retractions by authors with a single retraction, while lower barriers to retraction are indicated by the shorter time-to-retraction and the expansion of retraction criteria to include offenses such as plagiarism. These findings underscore the importance of continued vigilance in maintaining the integrity of the scientific record.
